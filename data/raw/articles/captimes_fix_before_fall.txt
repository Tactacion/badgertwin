The University of Wisconsin-Madison plans to repair recent air conditioning problems at campus buildings before students return later this summer, an administrator said July 16.
Fall semester classes are scheduled to start Sept. 2, with students set to move into dorms in late August.
About a month ago, a major chilled water line ruptured near the Charter Street Healing and Cooling Plant. The pipe was part of a system that provides air conditioning for much of campus, including academic buildings, research facilities and hospitals.
“The damaged section of pipe has been removed, and work is underway to install a permanent replacement,” Deej Lundgren, the UW system’s associate vice president for capital planning and budget, told the Board of Regents during a virtual meeting July 16.
UW-Madison continues to calculate the total cost of the repairs and determine what caused the break. The university may seek additional funding from the UW system in the future, Lundgren said. The pipe was 4 feet in diameter and buried 20 feet underground, which made it “very challenging to get at,” he said.
"In partnership with the state of Wisconsin, it was determined that the current Engineering Drive utility project could be used to support the repair efforts," Lundgren said.
UW-Madison is updating the utilities on the engineering campus before constructing a new facility . The broken chilled water line would have affected the ability to complete that project, said Lori Wilson, a spokesperson for the university's Division of Facilities Planning and Management.
UW-Madison has remained open since the pipe ruptured, but the university closed nearly three dozen buildings to the public. A list of those facilities, including the historic Bascom Hall and Science Hall, is available on the university’s website .

Some other buildings may feel warmer with higher levels of humidity as a result of the limited cooling capabilities. Memorial Union and Union South are open with air conditioning. Summer classes have continued but may have been relocated.
More than 18,000 students have typically enrolled in UW-Madison’s summer courses in recent years, according to university figures .
UW-Madison placed temporary chillers across campus to help with cooling. The Madison area was under a heat advisory this month, and temperatures reached the upper 90s, according to the National Weather Service.
The university has prioritized cooling capacity for patient care, research activities and other critical functions. Those efforts include the University Hospital, American Family Children's Hospital, William S. Middleton Memorial Veterans' Hospital and UW Veterinary Care, Lundgren said.

Regent Jim Kreuser said the situation at UW-Madison is indicative of aging infrastructure at many of Wisconsin’s public universities.
“We need to be prepared for this. … As things get older, these may happen with more frequency,” he said.
Kreuser and Edmund Manydeeds, a fellow regent, agreed that Wisconsin legislators should be aware of the current and potential issues state-funded campuses are experiencing. Fixing roofs and chilling systems are large investments, Manydeeds said, but “by not maintaining them, we’re committing negligence in our duties.”
“I think we should start being proactive in … having the campus identify their problem areas that they’re well aware of, and start trying to come up with a plan to resolve those issues,” Manydeeds said.
UW-Madison is “facing a tsunami of building issues quickly headed our way,” Cindy Torstveit, the university’s associate vice chancellor for facilities planning and management, previously told the Regents in February.
UW-Madison had two major building booms in the mid-20th century and again in the early 2000s. The university is approaching a point where renovations needs from both periods will overlap, she said.
“UW-Madison has approximately $2.5 billion of deferred maintenance, and while we have begun to address it, we need to do more,” Torstveit said at the time.
That total doesn't include underground utilities, Wilson said.
"Deferred maintenance does not mean UW-Madison is not maintaining campus facilities," she said. "This refers to the backlog of work or deferral of investment in work that is necessary when building components and systems reach the end of their natural life, even when well-maintained over time."
